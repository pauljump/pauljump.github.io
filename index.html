model training

itchy

what can a 16 MB model teach us about private, on-device ai?

I was working on a product whose core workflow analyzes extremely sensitive medical files. Keeping that analysis on the user's device, ideally an iPhone, would keep the files inside a clear privacy boundary. It would also make memory, speed, power use, and model quality core design constraints.

the test

OpenAI's Parameter Golf challenge gave me a fixed 16 MB limit for testing one part of that problem. My hypothesis was that a byte-level model could eliminate tokenizer and embedding overhead, then spend more of a fixed parameter budget on the network itself. This was not a clinical model or proof of iPhone viability. It was a focused test of whether byte-level input could improve quality under tight device limits.

what i built

A from-scratch language model, data pipeline, training loop, quantization path, local MLX implementation, CUDA training path, and a repeatable comparison harness. I trained and compared model variants rather than fine-tuning an existing foundation model.

the important failure

A patching experiment produced implausibly strong scores. I traced the data flow and found that 11 of every 12 target bytes were visible in the input. I invalidated the result publicly, removed the leak, and reran the same comparison fairly.

evaluation systems

local legal ai + harvey lab

how do you know when a legal ai system is wrong?

A small firm told me it was uncomfortable sending client material to cloud AI systems. I started with the same instinct: bring the model to the data. I ran Qwen3 locally on Apple Silicon and began adapting realistic legal tasks to local inference.

the more useful question

Running locally kept the data off the cloud, but did not give a lawyer a reason to trust the answer. The serious failures were often quieter than a made-up citation: a required issue left out, the wrong document treated as controlling, evidence overstated, or an uncertain record presented as a clean conclusion. Privacy got me started. Trust became the harder problem. That shifted the work from packaging local models to building the search, testing, and evidence checks around them.

what i built

I mapped the full Harvey LAB corpus, adapted its agent harness for local MLX inference, added routing and completion gates, and defined a method that locks the task, evidence, answer, and run record before the scoring rules are revealed.

what i tested

I piloted a credit-agreement-versus-term-sheet comparison: keyword search, semantic and hybrid reranking, pairwise and combined verification, evidence matching, and missing-issue checks across 13 defined provisions.

the shared pattern

models improve when the data, evaluation, and feedback loop become a system.

01define the task

Turn a real workflow into clear success and failure.

02lock the test

Keep development data, evidence, scoring rules, and final tests separate.

03measure failures

Separate search, evidence, missing-issue, model, output, and code errors.

04improve the system

Change data, retrieval, routing, verification, or the model only when evidence supports it.

the honest scope

I have trained models from scratch in Itchy. In the legal work, I did not fine-tune a model. I built and tested the surrounding ML system: local inference, benchmark adaptation, retrieval, evals, verification, source tracking, and repeatable tests. That is the work I would bring to an applied AI or forward-deployed team, alongside experience building and leading technical organizations.

The working pattern across both projects is simple: start with a blocked customer workflow, isolate the real technical constraint, build enough of the stack to test it, discard the attractive answer when the evidence disagrees, and leave behind a reusable quality loop.
