The freedom and the problem

AI let me make software faster than I could decide what deserved to exist.

Casting a wide net was not a failure to choose. It was how I learned to see the pattern. Every rabbit hole left behind a piece of the system I would eventually need.

Field notes from the search

Seven builds. Seven lessons. One direction.

The projects look unrelated from far away. Up close, they are all about giving one person the context, software, and leverage usually reserved for an institution.

01

Colosseum

Generation got cheap. Judgment did not.

I kept getting more honest answers when I made models argue than when I asked one model for advice. So I built a real-time debate arena with structured roles, provenance, human votes, and a separate judge.

What it taught meThe useful product is rarely the answer. It is the system that produces the answer, challenges it, and leaves receipts.

02

Unignorable

Institutions close tickets. People live with what comes back.

Problems around my kids' school kept getting reported to 311, closed, and reported again as if they were unrelated. I joined 455,000 reports into one history and mapped what the city kept forgetting.

What it taught meThe person on the other side of an institution needs memory stronger than the institution's workflow.

03

Pricefixed

Sometimes the missing product is actually a missing data layer.

Every real-estate idea I tried hit the same wall: the facts a renter needed were fragmented across listings, public records, price histories, and landlord portfolios. I joined them across millions of apartments.

What it taught meConsumer power starts by reconstructing the context that better-resourced institutions already have.

04

Unhinged

Personal software can be intimate without becoming extractive.

I wondered whether an iPhone and Apple Watch could tell you what your body already knew about a date. I built a private report from 16 on-device signals.

What it taught meThe most personal context can stay with the person. Useful AI does not require turning their life into somebody else's dataset.

05

RexAir + Walt's Workshop

Software for one person is a real market now.

My sons needed software built around how they actually think. I built Rex a real-flight learning system and gave Walt a voice-to-game loop that shipped 13 games from his own ideas.

What it taught meAI makes custom software cheap enough to begin with one person. The pattern that works for them can become the starting point for the next family.

06

Slopify

You can own more of the stack than you think.

A joke about Spotify deleting AI music became a streaming product, a locally generated catalog, and an iPhone app that could make music without a generation API.

What it taught meLocal and open models are not just cheaper infrastructure. They change which consumer products can exist and who gets to control them.

07

Teek + Vibecheck + Whetstone

Sounding right is not the same as being right.

I built source-backed models of how people decide, a private profiler of how I build, and evaluation harnesses that force AI systems to show where their claims came from.

What it taught meProvenance and evaluation cannot be a final polish step. In consequential workflows, they are the product.

What survived every experiment

The model is not the product.

The product is the context, workflow, and evaluation around it.

  1. 01
    Context before model

    Knowing what to look for matters more than asking a larger model.

  2. 02
    Workflow over chat

    A useful answer still leaves the person holding all the work.

  3. 03
    Receipts over vibes

    Sources, checks, and outcomes have to survive the demo.

  4. 04
    Every case compounds

    A solved edge should make the next person's problem cheaper to finish.

Then the work came home.

Kit is where the experiments stopped being experiments.

My son is autistic. For five years, my family has navigated schools, therapies, insurers, evaluations, and New York's OPWDD system. Money and support can be sitting there, technically available, while the parent becomes the integration layer between institutions that do not talk to each other.

At a dads' night, another parent told me a swimming reimbursement had been stuck for weeks. I pulled out my phone, researched the rule, the fiscal intermediary, and the pool, then built the missing provider evidence. The family told me the first Kit-prepared packet was approved within 48 hours.

I reused the workflow for martial arts and piano. All three families reported that their first Kit-prepared packet was approved. What looked like magic was a workflow. What worked once could become a pattern for the next family.

Forward deployment used to be reserved for enterprises because only enterprises could afford engineers inside their problems. AI changes that. Kit starts family by family, solves the workflow end to end, and turns what worked into something reusable.
