Applied AI product systems · 2026

I turn field work into product systems.

The model is rarely the hard part. The hard part is finding the shared problem inside a messy request, deciding what belongs in the product, and making the result trustworthy enough for a team to run with it. These are three examples of that work: a live service product, a legal AI reliability layer, and a small-model experiment built to answer a real constraint rather than follow the obvious architecture.

Start close to the field. Separate reusable work from case-specific judgment. Build the evidence, workflow, and measurement layer once. Keep a human accountable for the decision that only a human can make.

01

field work → product rail

Kit · New York OPWDD Self-Direction

Do the provider work before the family has to ask for it.

This began as a passion project with a nonprofit I met through my board work with the Simons Foundation. Families had money in a self-direction budget, but getting a class or service approved could still mean weeks of research, provider follow-up, and a packet built from scratch. The product opportunity was not another better form. It was to move the repeated provider-side work upstream.

What the field looked like

Every request started at zero

A family found something their child wanted to do. Then someone had to determine the funding route, find the actual offering, establish the provider facts, collect evidence, identify the gaps, and assemble a request for review. The same provider work repeated from one request to the next.

The product decision

Build a reusable provider evidence layer

Kit models an organization, location, specific offering, and reimbursement pathway separately. It preserves public sources, maps evidence to deterministic rules, records what is missing, and knows when the record needs a refresh. A family request then joins to that reusable dossier instead of restarting the work.

From one-off request to core product

Participant data stays outside the shared provider system.

01 Ingest the supply

Public and official sources, program catalogs, and provider pages feed a statewide discovery and normalization pipeline.

02 Build the dossier

Facts, excerpts, timestamps, and hashes attach to the exact offering and pathway instead of becoming an opaque score.

03 Route the request

Kit identifies the funding route, pre-fills what the evidence supports, and asks only for the facts a real person must add.

04 Review and learn

The family reviews every packet. Changed sources, missing provider facts, and outcomes become structured follow-up work.

The line that matters

Kit does not pre-approve providers and it does not decide whether a family is eligible or reimbursed. It pre-builds the participant- independent evidence and workflow work that otherwise happens ad hoc. The private Life Plan, budget, family decision, and final institutional approval join later and remain human-owned.

02

field signal → reliability layer

Local legal AI · Harvey LAB

Make trust a product capability, not a user’s leap of faith.

Kit led to a conversation with a small law firm that had banned AI firm-wide. Their concern was reasonable. Keeping client material local solves a privacy problem, not a trust problem. A private answer can still omit a required issue, ground itself in the wrong document, or express uncertainty as confidence.

What I did not build

A nicer legal chatbot

That would have made a fluent answer easier to produce and a serious error harder to see. The more useful question was what has to be true before an AI answer enters a real workflow.

The product decision

Separate the answer from the evidence around it

I mapped the workflow space, then built a testable loop around retrieval, reranking, evidence selection, missing-issue checks, and a locked scoring protocol. Each run leaves an inspectable record instead of a confidence score nobody can audit.

03

constraint → technical judgment

Itchy · OpenAI Parameter Golf

Start from the constraint, then benchmark the idea honestly.

The challenge was to build the strongest possible language model in a 16 MB artifact. The conventional answer was to shrink a token-based model. I took the constraint literally instead: use raw bytes, remove tokenizer overhead, and spend more of the fixed budget on the model. The question was not whether the architecture sounded clever. It was whether it could survive a fair comparison.

The nontraditional bet

Build small from birth

I built the model, data pipeline, training loop, quantization path, local MLX implementation, CUDA path, and comparison harness. A 256-byte vocabulary avoided the usual tokenizer and embedding overhead, leaving more of the 16 MB budget for the network itself.

The product-quality gate

Kill the exciting bad result

An early patching run looked exceptional. I traced the data flow and found that 11 of every 12 target bytes were visible in the input. I invalidated the result, removed the leakage, and kept the clean rerun beside the original experiment instead of optimizing around a false win.

More important, the evaluation caught the failure before it became a claim. This is how I want product teams to use technical evidence: as a decision tool, not a story generator.

The operating loop

The pattern I would bring to a product organization.

01 Get close to the work

Find the expensive exception, the real source of delay, and the failure users can see but cannot yet name.

02 Decide what becomes core

Separate reusable evidence and workflow from the customer-specific judgment that should stay with the customer.

03 Make it measurable

Instrument the system around the model, preserve the evidence, and make misses visible before they become product lore.

04 Feed the field back into product

Turn changed sources, missing facts, edge cases, and customer outcomes into a ranked core-product roadmap.

I am most useful where strategy, customers, product, and technical reality meet. The job is to make the loop tighter without making the system less honest.
